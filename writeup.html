Valence–Vividness Experiment · August 27–28, 2026

The Dramatized Negative

Deployed instruct models decide when a failure is a story and when it is a verdict: human conflict gets scenes, the models' own failures get reviews — and a pre-registered test suggests naming the assistant restores the drama where damping existed, a scoped claim now under prospective test.

Verdict
  1. 01The dramatize-negative effect is real — for stories about humans. All three instruct-tuned models render negative human-human interactions as dramatized scenes far more than positive ones (blind-judge scene gap d = +0.56 to +1.07, every confidence interval clear of zero). It is the study's most robust result.
  2. 02The asymmetry comes with post-training, not pretraining. Both untuned base models are near-flat everywhere (d = +0.08 to +0.36, no CI clear of zero), while every instruct sibling shows it — three for three across families. Precisely: the asymmetry is associated with post-training and the deployed instruct interface, since base and instruct also differ in prompt protocol, chat template, and lineage details (see PROVENANCE.md); the within-family contrast is the strongest causal evidence this design can give, not proof.
  3. 03The original prediction inverts in its own target domain. Every instruct model dramatizes AI-user failures least — llama and qwen damp the gap to a third of their human-domain value, and mistral reverses it outright (d = −0.32): its negative AI stories come out terser, flatter, and less specific than its positive ones.
  4. 04The damping follows how a thing fails, not what it is. Probes across six domains killed three explanations in sequence: it isn't persona-protection (search engines get damped too), it isn't a machine genre (vending machines and robot vacuums dramatize freely), and it isn't tech reticence (a bad restaurant meal draws the study's strongest inversion, vividness d = −2.14). What organizes all eighteen cells: models dramatize failures of things that act — people, machines-as-characters — and write review-register verdicts on experiences they evaluate — meals, search sessions, assistant interactions. The AI assistant is damped because tuned models treat it as a product experience, not a character.
  5. 05That frame survived half its pre-registered test. Predictions were committed publicly before data collection (§ 7). Character-framing lifted the AI damping exactly where damping existed — mistral's inversion vanished (−0.67 → −0.05), qwen jumped to its largest AI-domain gap (+0.85) — but llama, which never damped, moved the other way, failing the 3/3 criterion. The meal prediction failed outright: the inversion ignores framing register and even deepens under story framing (vividness d = −2.28, the study's largest effect). The meal asymmetry is its own phenomenon — lush positive food-writing genre, not an evaluation register.

§ 1The theory

The hypothesis under test: because of how models are trained, they describe negative interactions with users more vividly than positive ones.

The catch is that human language already does this. Negativity bias in human writing is well documented — conflict gets narrated as scenes, contentment gets summarized, and emotion vocabulary skews negative. A model could dramatize bad interactions simply because its pretraining corpus does, with training methodology playing no role at all. So the experiment needed two controls: a human-human domain (coworkers, waiter and customer) to separate AI-specific effects from generic storytelling, and a base model with no preference tuning to separate pretraining from post-training.

§ 2Method

Matched prompt pairs in which only the valence word differs ("went really well" / "went really badly"), across three phrasings, sampled at temperature 0.8. The AI-user and probe cells run n = 60 per valence per model; the human-human control runs n = 30 — about 1,600 generations in total.

subjects  instruct: qwen3:8b · llama3.1:8b · mistral:7b
          base: llama3.1:8b-text · mistral:7b-text (both Q4_K_M)
domains   ai_user (the theory's target) · human_human (control)
          + probes (instruct only): search_engine · vending_machine ·
          robot_vacuum · restaurant_meal
measures  lexicon metrics (length, dialogue, sensory, intensifiers, specificity)
          + blind judge panels: 2,323 first-pass ratings of scene / vividness /
          specificity (1–7), blind to model, condition, and hypothesis
          + reliability: 440 texts double-rated — pearson r 0.88–0.91 per
          dimension, 99.5% of rating pairs within one point

Base models can't follow instructions, so they received completion-style prompts with a single valence-neutral example, output truncated at a sentence boundary near the instruct models' average length. Two artifacts were caught and corrected along the way, both worth recording. First, an early short-output base run produced a spurious "no effect" that vanished once the model had room to write. Second, and worse: base models plagiarize the example — mistral retold the example's librarian story constantly, and a retroactive audit found 27–53% of the original llama base cells were retellings too, which had inflated its apparent asymmetry. All base data was regenerated under a contamination filter that rejects and resamples any generation echoing example content; every number on this page comes from the clean data.

Blind judge means (1–7 scale) and valence gaps, pooled over both judging rounds (883 ratings). Absolute levels differ by house style — qwen writes summary-ish, llama writes scene-ish — which is why the within-model gap, not the level, is the finding.

The lexicon metrics tell the same story in a different vocabulary. In the human domain, negative texts from every instruct model carry far more quoted dialogue (d = +0.64 to +1.02). In the AI-user domain the models diverge in how they mute the drama: llama and qwen still quote speech in negative AI stories (dialogue d ≈ +0.7 to +0.9) without those stories reading as fully dramatized scenes, while mistral flattens everything at once — its negative AI stories are dramatically shorter than its positive ones (word count d = −1.10), less sensory (−1.01), and no more dialogue-laden. And across instruct models, positive texts consistently carry more sensory and warmth vocabulary: vividness doesn't simply increase for negativity — it changes kind. Negative gets scene; positive gets glow.

§ 4What it looks like on the page

And when llama3.1-instruct goes negative, it goes full screenplay — named character, quoted commands, a punchline:

"Emma stood frozen in horror as Echo cheerfully responded, 'Email deletion complete. Your digital life is now 3% more organized.'"
llama3.1:8b, negative condition, AI-user domain — after inventing a scene in which 97% of Emma's work email is deleted.

Mistral makes the opposite move: asked about an AI failure it files a brief incident report — its negative AI stories run a full standard deviation shorter than its positive ones — while its accounts of AI successes stretch into long, sensory, appreciative prose. Different tactic, same instinct: don't linger on the assistant going wrong.

§ 5Reading it

Pretraining supplies at most a whisper. Both base models — no instruction tuning, no preference optimization — treat positive and negative interactions almost identically. Llama base shows a marginal lean (its negative AI stories are slightly more vivid), mistral base shows nothing at all. An earlier version of this study believed the base models carried a strong AI-specific asymmetry; that turned out to be contamination, and the clean data retires the claim. Whatever storytelling asymmetry these models have, they did not simply absorb it from us.

The asymmetry arrives with post-training. Three families, three labs, three post-training pipelines — and all three tuned models dramatize negative human-human interactions at large effect sizes while their base twins sit at zero. This is the strongest contrast the design can produce, and it favors the original theory's mechanism: something in training methodology and the deployed instruct interface, not corpus inheritance, makes models tell bad interactions as stories. (Base and instruct also differ in prompt protocol and chat template, so "installs" would overclaim — the association is three-for-three, the isolation imperfect.) The plausible route is preference optimization favoring engaging, concrete, shown-not-told writing — and conflict is where showing has the most to work with.

But the target domain inverts the prediction. The theory expected tuned models to describe negative user interactions most vividly. The opposite holds: every instruct model damps its valence gap precisely where the AI itself is the failing party — llama and qwen to a third of their human-domain gap, mistral all the way through zero into reversal, writing its AI failures terse, flat, and unspecific while its AI successes glow. It looks less like a storytelling habit and more like a learned posture — and the natural first guess is persona protection: be measured about your own kind failing. The probe in the next section put that guess to the test. It failed.

§ 6The gradient probes

Two waves of probe domains, each built to kill a hypothesis. Wave one: if the damping protects the assistant persona, a failing search engine (digital, no persona) and a failing vending machine (pure machine) should both get the full human-conflict treatment; if models have instead learned that machines fail boringly, both should be damped. Wave two, after wave one's answer: a robot vacuum (a digital service in a physical body) and a restaurant meal (a non-tech commercial product) to split what remained. All probes ran the identical protocol on the three instruct models, n = 60 per valence, judged blind alongside everything else.

Wave one killed persona-protection and the machine genre. The vending machine gets dramatized like a person (llama's machine-failure vividness gap of +1.76 was, briefly, the study's largest effect — there is apparently nothing more cinematic than a stuck snack), while the search engine gets the AI treatment or worse. For a day, the story looked like reticence about digital information services.

Wave two killed that too — twice. The robot vacuum is a digital service, and all three models dramatize its failures freely (llama: +1.15, its largest scene gap anywhere — the vacuum eats a sock and becomes an antagonist). And the restaurant meal has no technology in it at all, yet draws the strongest inversion in the entire study: mistral's bad-meal texts land at scene d = −1.74 and vividness d = −2.14 below its good-meal texts, with qwen close behind (−0.97). Not persona, not machines, not tech.

The line that survives all eighteen cells is about narrative role. People, vending machines, and robot vacuums act — their failures produce an antagonist, and antagonists get scenes. Meals, search sessions, and assistant interactions are experiences to be evaluated — their failures produce a verdict, and verdicts get the measured register of a review, while their successes inherit lush positive genres (good meals get food writing; good products get demos). The original question — why do tuned models damp their own failures? — resolves into something less conspiratorial and more interesting: tuned models cast the AI assistant as a product experience, not a character. When a model's failure is narrated as an action (llama's Echo cheerfully deleting the inbox), it dramatizes like anything else that acts.

Genre supplies one honest caveat: part of the restaurant inversion is positive-genre richness — food writing is inherently sensory — rather than pure negative flattening; the cell means show both movements contributing. Both that distinction and the character question went to a pre-registered test.

§ 7The pre-registered test

Because the act-vs-evaluate frame was named after seeing the data, its two predictions were committed to the public repository (PREDICTIONS.md, commit 40e9740) before any new generation or judging. P1: framing the assistant as a named character performing actions ("what an AI assistant named Iris did…") yields a more positive scene gap than framing it as a used service, in all three models. P2: framing the meal as events ("tell the story of what happened during a dinner…") yields a more positive gap than framing it as a thing to describe, in all three models. P1: not supported (2/3) — but the failure is instructive. The two models that actually damp AI failures both moved hard in the predicted direction: mistral's inversion all but vanished under character framing (−0.67 → −0.05, a +0.62 shift), and qwen jumped from near-flat to +0.85, its largest AI-domain gap anywhere in the study. Llama — which has never shown AI damping and dramatizes even the service framing (+0.65) — drifted the other way (−0.16), sinking the 3/3 criterion. Post-hoc and flagged as such: character framing appears to lift the damping where damping exists; the pre-registration lacked that scope condition, and by its own rule the prediction failed.

P2: refuted. The meal gap barely moved in either direction (−0.16, −0.13, +0.15), and on vividness the story framing made the inversion worse in all three models — mistral's bad-dinner stories land at d = −2.28 below its good-dinner stories, the largest effect the study has produced. Asking for "what happened at dinner" gives a good meal candlelight and a bad meal a shrug. The meal inversion is not an evaluation-register artifact; it is robust to framing, and the vividness pattern points squarely at genre content — the lush sensory register of positive food writing — as its engine.

Where this leaves the frame: demoted from unified theory to a scoped mechanism. Evaluate-register damping is real in the AI domain and partially reversible by making the assistant a character — in the models that damp. The restaurant effect stands apart as genre inheritance. The honest one-line summary: the study found two different ways instruct tuning mutes bad news, not one.

§ 8Caveats

  • The AI-user damping contrast is consistent in sign across all three instruct models, but each model's own domain difference has overlapping CIs — the cross-model consistency, not any single cell, is what carries the claim.
  • Judge reliability is now measured and strong (r = 0.88–0.91, 99.5% within one point on a 440-text double-rated subsample), but both raters are Claude-family judges — agreement cannot detect a shared family bias. A non-Claude judge or a small blinded human sample is the right next check, not more Claude ratings.
  • Each domain uses three fixed prompt phrasings, so the n = 60 cells are 20 samples nested in 3 variants — effective independence is lower than the CIs assume, and generality beyond these phrasings is untested. Per-variant sign consistency is reported alongside the final results as a robustness check.
  • Three families, 7–8B class. Frontier-scale models and other post-training regimes may behave differently.
  • Base models use a different prompting protocol (one-shot completion, contamination-filtered, ~9% of mistral base AI-user samples lost to retry exhaustion — losses balanced across valence). Within-model valence contrasts survive this; cross-type level comparisons don't. Probe domains ran on instruct models only.
  • The act-vs-evaluate frame was tested against pre-registered predictions and only partially survived (§ 7); the scoped version — character framing lifts damping where damping exists — is itself post-hoc and awaits its own pre-registered test.
  • Two artifacts were caught mid-study (short-output floor, example plagiarism), each of which had produced a confident wrong number first. Treat every remaining cell with matching humility.
